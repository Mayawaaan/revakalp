## History & Origin

Tie-resist dyeing (*bandhana*, Sanskrit for "to bind") appears in Indian visual records dating back over fifteen centuries, including Ajanta murals depicting speckled ceremonial garments. Across arid Marwar and Mewar, wearing radiant vermilion, turmeric, and saffron dyes evolved into a joyful visual celebration against desert landscapes.

Rajasthan's guild dyers (*rangrez*) and women tier artisans (*bandhanharis*) refined microscopic knotting to create luminous fields dotted like stars, while Kota nurtured gossamer *masuria* cotton-silk squares first commissioned by regional nobility.

## Regional Craft & Weaving Centers

Jodhpur, Jaipur, Bikaner, and Sikar lead Rajasthani Bandhej and Leheriya artistry—ranging from diagonal monsoon *Leheriya* and crisscrossing *Mothra* checks to festive *Pachranga* five-color celebratory drapes.

Meanwhile in Kaithoon near Kota, weavers construct translucent *Kota Doria* sarees using square-grid *khats* where cotton yarns supply structural strength and mulberry silk imparts understated radiance.

## Material Characteristics & Drape

For fine Bandhani and Leheriya, fluid bases like pure crepe silk, chinon, georgette, and featherlight *mulmul* absorb successive dye baths cleanly so untouched white centers bloom vividly when the threads are untied.

Freshly opened Bandhani often keeps faint miniature dimples around each pinched knot, giving the fabric a supple tactile bounce across the pallu and shoulder pleats.

## Craftsmanship & Loom Technique

In Bandhani, an artisan pinches minuscule peaks of damp cloth over a pointed fingernail or brass thimble and wraps continuous cotton thread tightly around every bud. Multi-stage dyeing moves from pale yellow to red, green, and deep burgundy by tying fresh knots between immersions.

For *Leheriya*, the yardage is twisted diagonally into a taut rope and bound at rhythmic intervals so overlapping dye baths form crisp, undulating diagonal ripples once unrolled.

## Cultural Significance

Across Rajasthan and Malwa, Leheriya marks the onset of Shravan and Teej monsoons, while crimson and saffron Bandhani—often paired with gleaming *gotapatti* appliqué—is treasured for weddings, Gangaur processions, and festive gifting.

## Care & Preservation Guidance

- Always dry-clean richly dyed Bandhani and Leheriya sarees so saturated pigments stay crisp without color migration.
- Avoid flattening soft Bandhani crinkles with heavy direct heat; light indirect steaming restores fluid movement.
- Wrap jewel-toned reds, emeralds, and indigos individually in neutral cotton bags inside dry storage.
- Shield metallic gotapatti borders from moisture and direct perfume spray.
